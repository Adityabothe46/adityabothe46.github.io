Quality systems  /  Case study

ISO 9001
Gap Analysis

Aluminium anodizing job shop, AB Enterprises

An assessment of an unregistered job shop operation against ISO 9001 requirements, based on direct experience running the process from 2021 to 2024. The operation was not certified and did not maintain a formal quality management system. This analysis identifies where existing practice aligned with clause requirements and where it fell short.

Why this is worth writing

Most small manufacturers are not certified and have no intention of becoming certified. That does not mean they have no quality system. It means the system lives in people rather than documents.

AB Enterprises ran on about ten people, with the owner on site daily and one engineer holding the process knowledge. Quality was the main selling point of the business, and the commitment behind that was real. Customers came back because the finish was consistent.

The business also ran its own aluminium extrusion plant, which is why most of the aluminium we processed was sourced in house. That plant ran about 4 to 5 tons a day at a 4 to 5 percent rejection rate, mostly section bending and temper defects. My involvement there was management oversight rather than process engineering: production scheduling, raw material and ingot inventory, on time delivery, and tracking the rejection rate. A dedicated team owned floor quality and operations.

What the operation did not have was the structure that lets a system survive without the people currently holding it. That is the gap this analysis describes, and it is a more useful thing to understand than a list of boxes ticked.

Scope and basis

This covers selected clauses across 4 to 10. Findings are based on recollection of operating practice rather than on retained evidence, since records were discarded after their retention period. Where a finding rests on practice I ran personally, it is stated as such. Where structure was absent, it is stated plainly.

Assessed against ISO 9001:2015, the edition in force during the period described. ISO 9001:2026 was published in September 2026 and supersedes it, with a three year transition. Clause 8.3, design and development, is scoped out: the shop worked to customer specification and carried out no design.

The conclusion is not that the operation was conformant. It was not. The conclusion is that a number of clause intents were being met through practice, and a smaller number were not being met at all.

Where the operation was genuinely strong

7.1.5.2 Measurement traceability

The standard requires that measuring equipment used to verify conformity be calibrated or verified at defined intervals against traceable standards, with records retained.

The DFT meter, hydrometer and TDS meter were all returned to the supplier for calibration at set intervals. This was the practice throughout, not something introduced after a problem.

This is the finding I would lead with, because it is the control most small shops skip. Coating thickness was the single characteristic customers paid for, and the instrument that measured it was verified rather than trusted.

8.5.3 Property belonging to customers

Most orders ran on extrusions from our own plant. A minority came in on material the customer had bought from an extruder of their choice, and that material stayed their property through all ten tanks.

Incoming customer material was checked on arrival for surface condition, dimensions and visible defects. Where something was wrong we flagged it before processing and gave the customer the choice of going ahead on their material or having us source a replacement. Material from the two sources was held as separate inventory.

The check and the customer's decision were written on the job card, and we called the customer to tell them what we had found before going ahead. That is what the clause asks for, including the requirement to report when property is found unsuitable. Retention of those job cards falls under the 7.5.3 finding below, where the period was de facto rather than defined.

The exposure sits in the process rather than the control. Over-etch cannot be reversed, so a process failure on a customer's own extrusions destroys their property rather than ours. The PFMEA ranks that failure mode at RPN 168.

Vertical integration as a quality control

Owning the extrusion plant meant the main input was made to our own standard rather than bought on spec. We did not compromise on quality at the extrusion stage either, and the anodizing line inherited the benefit. Where a customer chose their own extruder we had no such control, and that showed up as more quality problems on those orders.

8.7 and 10.2 Nonconforming output and corrective action

Every rejected load was written up. What failed, why, what was done with the material, and what would change to stop it happening again.

The clearest example was a customer order returned for thickness and colour non-conformance. I went to the customer site, investigated, produced a written report for the owner, and recovered the payment. Working through it afterwards, both defects traced to a single probable cause in the anodizing step, since thin coating produces shallow pores and shallow pores take up less dye.

That is what 10.2 asks for. Review the nonconformity, determine the cause, evaluate whether similar nonconformities exist, and act.

8.2.3 Review of customer requirements

A job card was raised for every order carrying the finish type, micron specification and colour. Requirements were established and recorded before work started, which is the substance of this clause.

8.5.1 Controlled conditions

Production ran to defined parameters. Dwell times per tank and per finish route, rectifier settings against calculated load surface area, bath chemistry checked weekly by hydrometer, DM water held at a defined TDS. Operators worked to checkpoints they had been trained on.

7.2 and 7.3 Competence and awareness

Associates and supervisors were trained on what to look for at each stage and on the quality commitment behind it. Cross training across skill areas was deliberate.

Where practice existed but structure did not

This is the largest category, and it is the honest description of the operation. The intent behind each clause was being served. The form the standard asks for was not there.

5.2 Quality policy

There was a real quality commitment, and it was communicated. The company motto was best in business, and the position was that quality would not be compromised even at the cost of taking a loss on an order. People were trained to it.

What was missing is the policy as a controlled document. Clause 5.2 asks for a policy that includes specific commitments, including to satisfying applicable requirements and to continual improvement, maintained as documented information, available to interested parties.

A commitment people understand is not the same as a document an auditor can read.

9.2 Internal audit

A review of the operation was carried out annually and findings were written up.

Clause 9.2 asks for more than that. An audit programme covering frequency, methods and responsibilities. Defined criteria and scope for each audit. Auditors selected to ensure objectivity, which in practice means not auditing your own work. Results reported to relevant management and records retained.

I audited a process I ran myself. That is useful and it is not independent.

9.3 Management review

Performance was reported to the owner, including business performance, customer feedback and resource needs. Being a two person leadership team with daily contact, that conversation happened constantly.

Clause 9.3 asks for review at planned intervals against a defined set of inputs, with documented outputs covering improvement opportunities, system changes and resource needs. Continuous informal review between two people is not the same thing, and it leaves no record.

8.4 Control of external providers

The main input, the aluminium itself, came mostly from our own extrusion plant rather than an external provider, so this clause applied mainly to chemicals, consumables and calibration services.

Those suppliers were selected on price, quality and material availability. Those are reasonable criteria and they were applied consistently.

What was absent is documented criteria for evaluation, selection and re-evaluation, and records of supplier performance. The judgement was sound. It was not written down, and it was not transferable.

7.5.3 Control of documented information

Job cards and rejection reports were kept for roughly two to five years and then discarded. That is a de facto retention practice rather than a defined one. The clause asks for retention periods to be determined and applied, with control over storage, protection and disposition.

6.2 Quality objectives

The objective was consistent quality, and rejection rate was known to run at two to three loads per hundred. That number was tracked in practice.

Clause 6.2 asks for objectives that are measurable, monitored, communicated and updated, with a plan stating what will be done, with what resources, by whom, by when, and how results will be evaluated. Knowing a number is not the same as managing against a target.

9.1 Monitoring, measurement and analysis

Thickness was verified, chemistry was checked, rejections were counted. What was not in place was analysis of that data over time. Thickness readings were taken on the first few loads of a day and not recorded load to load, so there was no trend data and no way to see drift before it became a reject.

8.5.2 Identification and traceability

Job cards identified the order. Beyond that there was no traceability from a finished batch back to the specific bath conditions, chemistry readings or operator on that load.

If a customer had come back six months later with a complaint, there would have been no way to reconstruct what happened to that batch.

Where there was no structure at all

4.1 to 4.4 Context of the organisation

Nothing documented. No stated scope of the quality system, no identification of interested parties and their requirements, no map of the processes and how they interact.

The process was understood. It was not written down as a system.

6.1 Actions to address risks and opportunities

Risk was handled by judgement. Experienced people knew which steps were fragile and watched them.

No structured risk analysis existed at the time. The PFMEA covering this process was produced later as a separate exercise, and the fact that it surfaced findings I had not previously identified is itself evidence that judgement alone was not reaching everything. Two of the highest ranked failure modes were ones nobody was watching for, because they could not be detected before the material left the floor.

What this says about the operation

The pattern is consistent. Where a control protected the product directly and the benefit was immediate, it was in place and it was maintained. Calibration, parameter control, rejection analysis, customer specification capture, incoming checks on customer material.

Where a control protected the system rather than the product, and the benefit was only visible over time or to someone outside the business, it was absent or informal. Documented scope, independent audit, structured review, supplier evaluation records, traceability.

That is a rational allocation of effort for a ten person shop with the owner on site. The things that stop bad material reaching a customer were covered. The things that let a system run without the people currently holding it were not.

The exposure is specific rather than general. The operation worked because two people held the whole picture between them and were present every day. It would not have survived either of them leaving, a doubling in volume, or a customer requiring documented evidence rather than a good finish.

What I would do differently

Record thickness readings load to load. The single highest value change. It converts inspection into trend data, makes drift visible before it becomes a reject, and creates the traceability that 8.5.2 asks for. Barely more work than the sampling scheme that was in place.

Write the quality policy down. The commitment was real and people understood it. Putting it in a controlled document costs an afternoon and turns a culture into something transferable.

Separate the auditor from the process. Even in a small shop, having the owner audit the engineer's process and the engineer audit the owner's would have been more honest than auditing my own work.

Document supplier criteria. Price, quality and availability were already the basis. Writing them down with a performance record makes the next person's decisions as good as mine were.

Hold management review on a schedule. The conversation was happening anyway. Putting a date on it, covering a defined agenda and writing down what was decided would have cost almost nothing.

Notes on method

This is a gap analysis, not an audit. An audit verifies against retained evidence. The records here were discarded after their retention period, so findings rest on recollection of practice I ran personally. Where that is the case the document says so rather than implying evidence exists.

The purpose was to understand how an operation I know well maps onto a standard I did not know at the time, and specifically to see which clause intents were being met without the vocabulary attached. More were than I expected. The ones that were not are the ones that would have mattered as the business grew.
