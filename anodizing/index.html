Anodizing Line
Process Analysis

PFMEA and Control Plan, AB Enterprises

Process analysis of an aluminium anodizing line I ran from 2021 to 2024, documented using PFMEA and control plan methodology.

About the operation

AB Enterprises is an aluminium anodizing job shop in Ahilyanagar, Maharashtra. Customers specify a finish, a coating thickness in microns, and a colour. We process to that spec and ship back.

The business also ran its own aluminium extrusion plant, which is why most of the aluminium we processed was sourced in house. That plant ran about 4 to 5 tons a day at a 4 to 5 percent rejection rate, mostly section bending and temper defects. My involvement there was management oversight rather than process engineering: production scheduling, raw material and ingot inventory, on time delivery, and tracking the rejection rate. A dedicated team owned floor quality and operations.

I set up the newer of the two plants and ran production across both. That covered sourcing raw material, scheduling orders, setting process parameters, checking bath chemistry, verifying coating thickness, maintaining the equipment, and training the people on the floor.

The line has ten tanks, each 21 feet long, 2.5 feet wide and 5 feet deep.

Supporting equipment is a 9 ton chiller with a cooling tower, an anodizing rectifier running 13 to 17 volts at 1600 to 1700 amps, a colour rectifier rated 500 amps and 200 volts, two buffing machines, an overhead crane, and a packing machine.

DM water is held at a TDS of 2 to 3. The chiller and cooling tower exist because the anodizing reaction puts a lot of heat into the sulfuric bath, and if the bath gets too warm the coating comes out soft and porous instead of hard.

Three process routes

The finish the customer asks for changes the route, and in one case changes the tank order. This matters because it is the main thing that can go wrong before any chemistry is involved.

Glossy

Buff first, then jig, then into the tanks.

  1. Buffing, polish buff
  2. Jigging, tie sections to vertical rods using 12.5 to 13 gauge aluminium wire
  3. Caustic, short dwell
  4. Nitric
  5. Anodize
  6. Colour
  7. Cutting, cut the wire and release the sections
  8. Drying
  9. Polishing on the buffing machine with a polish buff
  10. Packing

Normal matte

Same sequence, with two differences. Surfin buff instead of polish buff at the start, longer dwell in caustic, and no polishing after drying.

Hard matte

No buffing at any point, and the first two tanks run in reverse order.

  1. Jigging
  2. Nitric, 5 minutes, oil removal
  3. Caustic, 45 to 60 minutes
  4. Nitric again, 15 minutes, oil removal and brightening
  5. Anodize
  6. Colour
  7. Cutting
  8. Drying
  9. Packing

Silver skips the colour tank entirely, since the natural anodized finish is already silver.

The caustic dwell time is what separates glossy from matte. Short dwell leaves the surface smooth enough to polish to a shine. Long dwell etches it to a matte texture, and once that is done you cannot polish it back.

Loads run in parallel. While one sits in anodizing, another is in caustic and a third is being jigged. So throughput is not set by the 98.5 minute lead time, it is set by whichever station takes longest. That is anodizing at 25 minutes.

Over a 12 hour shift that works out to roughly 29 loads, which matches the 992 to 1050 kg we actually produced. Running two shifts gets us to about 2 tons a day. The older plant managed around 700 kg on an 8 hour shift with smaller tanks.

Micron spec drives capacity

Because anodizing is the constraint, and anodizing time scales with the coating thickness, the customer's micron spec sets how much we can put through the line.

Coating specAnodize timeLoads per 12 hApprox output
12 microns25 min291000 kg
18 microns45 min16560 kg
20 microns60 min12420 kg

A 20 micron order takes more than half the capacity out of the line compared to a 12 micron order. We charged a flat rate of about 50 rupees per kg regardless, which meant thick coating work earned the same per kilo while consuming far more of the bottleneck. Looking at it this way, the rate card should have been tied to micron spec rather than weight alone.

Drying takes 30 minutes, nearly a third of the lead time, and it varies with weather. It does not limit throughput because loads dry in parallel, but it does drive how much material is sitting on the floor at any time.

The absence of structured risk analysis at the time is covered in the ISO 9001 gap analysis, under clause 6.1.

What the analysis showed

The two seal rows score high on severity and detection but low on occurrence. We had no customer complaints about fading, bleeding or corrosion across the whole period. That tells me the seal process was running well, not that we had it under control. There was no way to verify a seal before material left the floor. The process held because the chemistry and timings were stable, which is a reasonable position while nothing changes, and a real exposure if volume, chemistry or staffing shifted.

Bath temperature carries the same shape of risk. The chiller worked, so it rarely bit, but there was no logged temperature record per load and no alarm. A soft coating passes every check we had.

Colour sits near the top on evidence rather than judgment. Our actual rejections were mostly colour, and I set occurrence to 6 to reflect that rather than the 4 I would have guessed.

The one escape we had

A commercial vehicle manufacturer's order came back with both thickness and colour out of spec. I went to the site, investigated, and recovered the full payment.

Working through it afterwards, the two defects were probably one problem. Colour uptake depends on the pore structure the anodizing step creates. Thin coating means shallow pores, shallow pores absorb less dye, and the result reads as a light or off shade. So the colour complaint may have been downstream evidence of the thickness problem rather than a colour tank failure.

That matters for the rejection data. Some share of the colour rejections we were writing off at 2 to 3 percent could have been anodizing issues showing up at the colour stage.

It also exposed the sampling scheme. We checked 2 to 3 sections on the first few loads of the day, and once the readings came back good we stopped checking for the rest of the shift. That design verifies the setup. It does not catch anything that develops during the day, such as bath temperature climbing through the afternoon, concentration drifting as loads accumulate, or a load with unusually high surface area getting the standard current.

Changes this analysis points to

Thickness checks spread across the shift. One section per load, every load, instead of 2 to 3 sections clustered on the first few loads. That is barely more work and it covers the whole day instead of just the setup. Recording the readings also turns inspection into trend data, which would show drift before it becomes a reject.

Measure at the worst case. Lowest point on the rack or furthest from the cathode, where current density is weakest, rather than wherever is easiest to reach.

Log bath temperature per load with an alarm limit. This is the single highest value change. It takes the detection score on soft coating from 8 down to around 3 and moves the risk from 216 to roughly 81.

Sampled seal testing. ASTM B136 or B680 on a per batch basis. We had no complaints, but we also had no way to know.

Hold period after sealing. Cold seal keeps reacting for a day or two after the tank. Recording the seal completion date on the batch record and releasing only after the hold would close that gap.

Finish route on the job card. Carrying the finish type with the load rather than relying on operator memory. Cheap to do, and the failure it prevents cannot be corrected once caustic has etched the surface.

Price against the constraint. Anodizing time scales with micron spec, and anodizing is the bottleneck. A flat per kilo rate undercharges thick coating work relative to what it costs in capacity.

Notes on method

Severity is set by the effect on the customer and cannot be lowered without changing the spec or the design. Occurrence comes down through prevention controls. Detection comes down through detection controls. The two are listed separately because they act on different columns.

Detection is scored inversely. A 1 means the defect cannot escape, a 10 means it cannot be caught in house.

The current AIAG VDA handbook uses an Action Priority rating rather than RPN. RPN is kept here because it is still what most plants talk in, and the ranking comes out the same either way.

Occurrence scores were revised after reviewing actual rejection records. My initial judgment put colour mid-table. The production data put it near the top, and the scores were corrected to match.
